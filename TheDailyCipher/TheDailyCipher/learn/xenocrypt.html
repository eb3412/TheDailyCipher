01 • OVERVIEW

What is the Spanish Xenocrypt?

A Xenocrypt is a substitution cryptogram whose plaintext is in a language other than English. In Codebusters-style practice, Spanish is the important case.

The cryptographic logic is the same as an Aristocrat, but your language clues must come from Spanish rather than English. The Daily Cipher treats Ñ as a distinct letter in its Spanish alphabet.

Competition note

The cipher mechanics on this page are self-contained. Science Olympiad event formats, allowed variants, and tournament constraints can change by season; the current official Rules Manual and official clarifications take precedence.

What you need to know

  • Accented vowels are normalized in The Daily Cipher practice text, while Ñ remains distinct.
  • Use Spanish common words, endings, and letter frequencies rather than English assumptions.

04 • ENCRYPTION

How encryption works

1

Preserve Ñ; accents may be normalized according to the puzzle format.

2

Build the substitution rows

Use the selected K1/K2 style and the supplied/constructed keyword.

3

Substitute consistently

Each Spanish plaintext letter maps to exactly one ciphertext letter.

4

Keep word boundaries if the mode does

This preserves valuable Spanish word-length clues.

05 • DECRYPTION

How decryption works

1

Identify short Spanish words

DE, LA, EL, EN, UN, ES, Y and similar forms are useful anchors.

2

Use endings

Plural -S/-ES and endings such as -CIÓN can reveal structures.

3

Maintain a one-to-one mapping

Exactly as with an Aristocrat.

4

Read as Spanish

Do not force English word patterns onto the plaintext.

06 • COMPETITION WORKFLOW

How to approach it in Codebusters practice

  • A Spanish vocabulary reference sheet can be more valuable than raw frequency tables.
  • Treat Ñ as its own letter when the problem convention does so.
  • An English keyword used to construct an alphabet is a key-building detail, not evidence that the plaintext is English.
07 • CRYPTANALYSIS

How to attack an unknown or partially known key

  • Spanish E and A are common, but frequency varies strongly by message.
  • DE and LA are especially useful short words when spaces are present.
  • Look for grammatical agreement: repeated endings can suggest plurals, gender, or verb forms.
